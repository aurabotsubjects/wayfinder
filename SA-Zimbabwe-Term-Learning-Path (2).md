# SKYBOUND: The Baobab Route
### A Term-Long Adventure Learning Path — South Africa & Zimbabwe
**For Years 5–6 (ages 10–12) — 10 Week Term — Monday to Friday**

*Companion planning document, built in the same spirit as your Wayfinder's Journal programme: one ongoing story running underneath five linked subjects, so students experience the term as a single adventure rather than five separate classes.*

---

## 1. THE BIG IDEA

Just as *Tama* pulled your class into the story of Pacific wayfinding, **Skybound** pulls them into the landscapes, history, wildlife and culture of Zimbabwe and South Africa — through the eyes of two local kids who become the class's guides for the term.

**The device:** In Week 1, the class discovers a battered leather logbook tucked inside an old suitcase (a prop, or simply revealed in a story-read) — the flight log of a retired bush pilot, **Sekuru Josiah**. Whenever it's opened, the classroom window fills with cloud, the room seems to lift and bank, and for the lesson the class is "flying" the Baobab Route with Josiah's grandchildren, who now keep the logbook and use it to bring visitors to meet their world. It's never over-explained — just delightfully real to the class, the same way Tama's journal worked.

Every **Monday** the logbook opens onto a new leg of the route (History). The other four days stay grounded in that same stretch of country — its land (Geography), its life (Science), the technology protecting that life (Technology), and its art (Art) — so the whole week feels like one place, seen from five angles.

## 2. THE TWO GUIDES

### TAPIWA — "we have been given" (Shona)
**Age 12 · Hwange / Victoria Falls, Zimbabwe**

Tapiwa lives on the edge of Hwange National Park. His father is an anti-poaching scout; the family also keeps a small maize and tobacco plot, so Tapiwa is equally at home talking elephants and talking crops. He's the joker of the two — copes with worry by cracking a joke a half-second too early — but underneath it he's genuinely anxious about what's happening to the wildlife around his home. He knows the bush the way Tama knows the sea.

- **Family:** His dad, Baba Farai (ranger); his mum runs the farm plot; his grandfather **Sekuru Josiah**, the retired bush pilot who taught both cousins to read a map and a sky, lives with them.
- **Flaw to grow across the term:** He rushes to "fix" problems before fully understanding them — gets him into a scrape or two, and by the final weeks he's learned to slow down and look first.
- **Recurring object:** A carved wooden elephant, whittled by Sekuru Josiah, that he keeps in his pocket and turns over when he's nervous.
- **Voice quirk:** Opens messages with "Sharp, crew!" (Zimbabwean slang for "all good/hi"), and has a habit of narrating danger as if it's less serious than it is — "It's fine, it's fine, it's mostly fine" — right before it very much isn't.

### NOMVULA — "born during the rain" (Zulu, a blessing-name)
**Age 11 · a Ndebele village, near the Zim/SA border area**

Nomvula is Tapiwa's cousin (their mothers are sisters) — she lives with her grandmother, **Gogo Thandiwe**, a master beadworker and one of the village's house-painters, in a village known for its brilliant geometric wall art. Nomvula is quieter than Tapiwa but far more stubborn — once she's decided something matters, she doesn't let it go. She sees the whole world in colour and pattern, and is the one who keeps pulling the class's attention to what things *mean*, not just what they look like.

- **Family:** Gogo Thandiwe (grandmother, master artist); her mother works in Johannesburg during the week and comes home on weekends, which Nomvula doesn't love but has made peace with.
- **Flaw to grow across the term:** She assumes she already knows the answer and stops listening too early — softens across the term as the class teaches her things about their own world too.
- **Recurring object:** A half-finished beadwork bracelet she's making, one bead added most weeks, that becomes visibly closer to finished by Week 10.
- **Voice quirk:** Ends important thoughts with "That's the pattern of it" — her grandmother's phrase for "that's the deeper meaning."

**Together:** Tapiwa flies the story forward (history/geography/wildlife); Nomvula grounds it in meaning (culture/art/food). Neither one is the expert — like Tama, they are learning some of this *with* the class, especially the harder history chapters, which keeps the fiction honest rather than a lecture in disguise.

**Hard rules (keep consistent, same spirit as the Tama bible):**
1. Neither character ever has all the answers — the Teacher Input sections carry the facts; Tapiwa and Nomvula carry the story and the feeling.
2. Age-appropriate emotional range only: excitement, worry, homesickness, pride, humour, determination. No romance, nothing graphically distressing — hard history (colonisation, apartheid) is presented honestly but through consequence and courage, not violence.
3. Every week, reference at least one continuing thread — the elephant carving, the bracelet, Sekuru Josiah, one of the two flaws — so they read as continuing people, not new masks each week.
4. They are always "the crew," "friends," or "you two" to each other — never call the class "students" in-story.

## 3. THE EMOTIONAL ARC ACROSS THE TERM

- **Weeks 1–2:** Wonder and welcome. Tapiwa and Nomvula are proud to show off home ground — Hwange, the Falls, the savanna.
- **Weeks 3–4:** The story gets heavier — poaching pressure on rhino, drought stress on farms. First real worry from Tapiwa; first time he says something like "I don't know if we can fix this one."
- **Week 5 (Boss Battle 1 — *The Lost City Trial*):** The class helps piece together the deep history (Great Zimbabwe, Mapungubwe, the Ndebele migration) to solve a challenge. Turning point — Tapiwa starts sounding a little more like a guide, less like a worried kid.
- **Weeks 6–7:** The route crosses into South Africa — bigger cities, harder history (colonisation, apartheid). Nomvula takes the lead here; this is where she's most vulnerable, since it's her family's history. A gentle, honest treatment: courage, unity and Ubuntu, not despair.
- **Weeks 8–9:** Warmth returns — food, farming's future, cattle culture, conservation success stories. Reflective tone; both guides start talking like people who could one day pass this on to someone else, the way Sekuru Josiah passed it to them.
- **Week 10 (Boss Battle 2 — *Legacy Trial*):** The class is formally named — Tapiwa and Nomvula call them "Route-Keepers" for the first time and mean it. Emotional payoff of the term.

## 5. WEEK-BY-WEEK DETAIL

Each day below gives: the **NZC learning area & Level 3–4 link**, a sample **learning intention**, and the **story tie-in** for that day. Use this as the planning skeleton — each entry can be expanded into a full lesson plan in the same template as your existing Term 2 files (Learning Intentions → Teacher Input → Main Content → Story Beat → Quest-log/Exit Ticket).

### WEEK 1 — Take-Off
*Story: The class finds the logbook. First flight opens over the Hwange bush at sunset — Tapiwa and Nomvula introduce themselves and the whole route ahead.*

- **Mon (History)** — *Social Sciences, Understanding Place & Environment.* WALT locate Zimbabwe and South Africa and understand who the San (Bushmen) were as Southern Africa's first peoples.
- **Tue (Geography)** — *Social Sciences, Understanding Place & Environment.* WALT describe Southern Africa's climate zones and how they shape where people live.
- **Wed (Science)** — *Living World, Ecology.* WALT describe the savanna biome and how the baobab tree survives extreme dry seasons.
- **Thu (Technology)** — *Technological Practice / Nature of Technology.* WALT understand that tracking technology has a long history — from San trackers reading spoor (footprints, broken grass, dung) to the radios, GPS units and cameras rangers use today — and why "reading the land" is still the first step in protecting animals.
- **Fri (Art)** — *Developing Practical Knowledge/Ideas.* WALT explore San rock art as one of the world's oldest storytelling art forms and create a symbol-based artwork of our own.

### WEEK 2 — Landing at Hwange
*Story: The crew visits Great Zimbabwe's ruins in a flashback told by Sekuru Josiah — Tapiwa is proud, a little awed by his own country's history.*

- **Mon** — Great Zimbabwe: the Stone City, its stone walls built without mortar, and what it tells us about a powerful trading civilisation before European contact.
- **Tue** — Subsistence farming: small-holder crops (maize, sorghum, groundnuts) and why they matter to family food security.
- **Wed** — Plant adaptation: how thorns, small leaves and deep roots help savanna plants survive drought.
- **Thu** — Communication technology: how two-way radios (like the ones Sekuru Josiah once used in the air, and Baba Farai still uses on patrol) let rangers coordinate across reserves the size of small countries — and why fast communication is often what saves an animal's life.
- **Fri** — Shona stone sculpture — Zimbabwe's internationally renowned art form; carving as storytelling.

### WEEK 3 — Following the Zambezi
*Story: The route follows the river toward Victoria Falls — Nomvula radios in with news from her village for the first time.*

- **Mon** — Mapungubwe: the gold and ivory trading kingdom, and Indian Ocean trade links stretching to Arabia, India and China.
- **Tue** — Commercial farming: tobacco, citrus and sugar — how farming for export differs from farming for the family table.
- **Wed** — Predators of the savanna: lion and leopard, and how food webs connect plants, herbivores and predators.
- **Thu** — Camera trap technology: motion- and heat-triggered cameras that let researchers study lions, leopards and other predators without ever disturbing them — and how the images they capture feed real conservation research.
- **Fri** — Ndebele house painting: the geometric wall art of Nomvula's village, and what the patterns can represent.

### WEEK 4 — Storm Over the Reserve
*Story: Tapiwa's dad radios that poachers have been spotted near the reserve. First real worry in the story — "I don't know if we can fix this one."*

- **Mon** — The Ndebele Kingdom and the Mfecane migrations of the early 1800s — how conflict and drought pushed large movements of people across the region.
- **Tue** — Water and irrigation: the Kariba Dam, and how drought affects farming communities.
- **Wed** — Endangered species: the rhino poaching crisis, and the science and people working to protect them.
- **Thu** — Anti-poaching technology: horn notching, microchipping and the RhODIS DNA database that lets every rhino horn in South Africa be traced — the real tools rangers like Tapiwa's dad use in the fight the family is living through this week.
- **Fri** — Beadwork: colour and pattern as a language — what different bead colours and patterns can communicate.

### WEEK 5 — BOSS BATTLE 1: The Lost City Trial
*Story: The crew must piece together clues from Great Zimbabwe, Mapungubwe and the Ndebele migrations to help solve a challenge blocking the route forward — a genuine turning point for Tapiwa's confidence.*

- A consolidation "trial" day/week across all five strands — quiz-adventure format, team challenges, and a class debrief connecting the four weeks of history, land, wildlife, ranger technology and art into one picture of the region before written history and colonisation. The Thursday leg is the **Ranger Tech Challenge**: teams design a simple monitoring or tracking solution — drawing on tracking, radios, camera traps and DNA tracking from Weeks 1–4 — for a chosen animal, then present and defend their design.

### WEEK 6 — Crossing the Border
*Story: The route crosses into South Africa — bigger cities, a different rhythm of life. Tapiwa is a little out of his depth here; Nomvula takes the lead for the first time.*

- **Mon** — Colonisation: the arrival of European settlers, the British South Africa Company, and its impact on land and people — presented honestly, focused on consequence rather than graphic detail.
- **Tue** — Land and food security: how land use changed, and what that meant for farming communities.
- **Wed** — Elephants as a keystone species: herd structure, migration and their outsized role in shaping the savanna.
- **Thu** — GPS and satellite tracking collars: how researchers follow elephant herds' movement patterns, and how the same technology gives early warning of human-elephant conflict before it happens.
- **Fri** — Township art and mural storytelling: how communities use public art to tell their own stories.

### WEEK 7 — The Village of Gogo Thandiwe
*Story: The crew lands in Nomvula's village. This is her chapter — proud, a little vulnerable, since some of this history is her own family's story.*

- **Mon** — Apartheid and the struggle for freedom: Nelson Mandela, the idea of Ubuntu ("I am, because we are"), and the path to reconciliation — framed around courage and unity, age-appropriate.
- **Tue** — Livestock farming and Nguni cattle: their deep cultural significance beyond farming — status, dowry (lobola), and identity.
- **Wed** — Pollinators: sunbirds and weaver birds, and their role in plant reproduction.
- **Thu** — Beehive fences: a simple, low-cost invention — real beehives strung on wire around fields — that uses elephants' natural fear of bees to protect both crops and elephants. Technology that solves a problem for people and animals at once, and ties straight back to Wednesday's pollinators.
- **Fri** — Weaving and textiles: Shweshwe fabric and basket weaving techniques.

### WEEK 8 — Independence Day
*Story: A celebration chapter — the crew joins in commemorations, music and food; a happier, prouder tone after Week 7's heavier history.*

- **Mon** — Independence: Zimbabwe's 1980 independence and South Africa's first democratic election in 1994 — what democracy and self-determination meant for each country.
- **Tue** — Farm to table: markets and supply chains, linking back to earlier farming units.
- **Wed** — Decomposers: the dung beetle's essential (and surprisingly funny) role in the savanna ecosystem.
- **Thu** — Modern ranger technology: drones for aerial patrols, thermal cameras for night work, and patrol-management software like the tools real Southern African parks use to plan where rangers go and log what they find.
- **Fri** — Music and dance as art: marimba, drumming and gumboot dance as expressive traditions.

### WEEK 9 — Looking Ahead
*Story: Reflective chapter — Tapiwa and Nomvula start to sound like they could one day pass all this on themselves, the way Sekuru Josiah passed it to them.*

- **Mon** — Southern Africa today: modern cities, rural life, and the diaspora — young people living between two worlds.
- **Tue** — Sustainable and climate-smart farming: conservation agriculture and adapting to a changing climate.
- **Wed** — Conservation success stories: national parks and community conservancies protecting wildlife today.
- **Thu** — Planning the Conservation Tech Expo: applying the technological design process (plan, create, evaluate) to invent a device or system that could help protect an endangered Southern African animal of the class's choice.
- **Fri** — Mask and carving traditions across the region.

### WEEK 10 — BOSS BATTLE 2: The Legacy Trial
*Story: The final chapter. Tapiwa and Nomvula formally call the class "Route-Keepers" — the emotional payoff of the whole term.*

- **Mon** — Timeline synthesis: the class builds a full timeline of everything the route has taught them, from the San to today.
- **Tue** — Present a farming plan for the future, drawing on every farming type studied.
- **Wed** — Design a conservation plan for an endangered species of choice.
- **Thu** — Conservation Tech Expo showcase: the class presents (and where possible builds or models) the protection technology they've designed, applying the whole term's Technology learning to a real conservation problem — the engineering half of Wednesday's conservation plan.
- **Fri** — Create a final "Baobab Route" artwork, drawing on every art form studied across the term, as a class legacy piece.

## 6. CHRISTIAN FAITH INTEGRATION

*Built to slot into your existing faith-connections structure (the same `ponder / verse / ref / value / valueDesc / questions[3]` shape used across Term 2), so this term reads as one continuous discipleship journey alongside the academic one.*

### Our Four Values, Applied to This Journey

- **Aroha — Love: God, Others, Self, World.** Shows up whenever the class meets a person, a place, or a creature worth caring about — Tapiwa's elephants, Nomvula's village, the land itself.
- **Mōhio — Know: God, His story and our place in it.** Shows up in every history and origins lesson — Southern Africa's story is part of the one bigger story God is telling across all nations.
- **Manaaki — Serve: Faithfully with our gifts and talents.** Shows up wherever craft, invention, or hospitality appear — weaving, designing ranger technology, the Conservation Tech Expo — serving others (and God's creatures) with what God has given.
- **Pānga — Impact: Bringing God's Kingdom to earth.** Shows up wherever the class is asked to act — conservation, sustainable farming, justice — small hands doing Kingdom work now.

### Our Motto: *Soli Deo Gloria* — To God Alone the Glory

Woven in as a closing thread: every boss battle and every "showcase" moment (the Conservation Tech Expo, the final artwork) is framed as an offering — not performance for its own sake, but skill and effort given back to God. Week 10 closes the whole term on this note deliberately.

### Three Worked Examples (Template for the Remaining Weeks)

**Week 1, Monday — The San People, Southern Africa's First Peoples**
- **Value:** Mōhio — Know
- **Ponder:** Long before any map was drawn, God had already placed the San people here, painting their world onto rock. Every nation's story — including this one — is part of the larger story God has been telling since the beginning.
- **Verse:** "From one man he made all the nations… and marked out their appointed times in history and the boundaries of their lands."
- **Ref:** Acts 17:26 (NIV)
- **valueDesc:** Learning where the San people came from helps us know God's story, and see that every people group has a place within it.
- **Questions:**
  1. If God marked out appointed times and places for every nation, what does that tell us about the San people — and about the country you were born in?
  2. Tapiwa and Nomvula are proud of where they come from. What are you proud of about where *you* come from?
  3. How does knowing God as the one who placed every nation change the way we look at a country very different from our own?

**Week 5, Monday — Boss Battle 1: The Lost City Trial**
- **Value:** Mōhio — Know
- **Ponder:** Joshua told the Israelites to stack up stones as a memorial, so that when their children asked "what do these stones mean?" the story of what God had done would never be forgotten. Great Zimbabwe's stone walls do something similar — they make sure a story is never lost.
- **Verse:** "In the future, when your children ask you, 'What do these stones mean?' tell them…"
- **Ref:** Joshua 4:6–7 (NIV, shortened)
- **valueDesc:** Piecing together Great Zimbabwe, Mapungubwe and the Ndebele migration helps us know that every civilisation's story matters to God, even the ones that were never written down.
- **Questions:**
  1. Why do you think God cares about people remembering their history, not just living in the present?
  2. What "stones" — objects, stories, photos — does your own family keep to remember its story?
  3. Tapiwa says the trial only works if the crew works together. How is that true of God's people too?

**Week 10, Friday — Boss Battle 2 Finale: The Baobab Route Artwork**
- **Value:** Aroha & the whole-term motto, *Soli Deo Gloria*
- **Ponder:** After a whole term of learning, making and discovering, this final artwork isn't about being praised for talent — it's about offering the best of what we've learned back to God, the way Nomvula's grandmother says every good pattern points back to its maker.
- **Verse:** "So whether you eat or drink or whatever you do, do it all for the glory of God."
- **Ref:** 1 Corinthians 10:31 (NIV)
- **valueDesc:** Bringing together everything learned this term into one final piece is a chance to practise Manaaki — using our gifts — for God's glory alone, not our own.
- **Questions:**
  1. Why do you think our school motto is "To God Alone the Glory" and not "to me"?
  2. Tapiwa and Nomvula now call the class "Route-Keepers." What is God calling *you* to keep and pass on?
  3. Looking back over the whole term — Aroha, Mōhio, Manaaki, Pānga — which one did you grow in the most, and why?

## 7. NZC LINKS USED THROUGHOUT (Years 5–6 / Level 3, stretching into Level 4 where noted)

- **Social Sciences:** Understanding the Past (perspectives, evidence); Understanding Place and Environment (exploration, consequences, resource use); Understanding How Societies Work (government, decision-making — independence/democracy weeks).
- **Science — Living World:** Life Processes, Ecology (interdependence, adaptation), Evolution (introductory, via extinction risk).
- **Technology:** Technological Practice (planning, making, evaluating) and Nature of Technology, through a Conservation & Field Technology focus (tracking, monitoring, communication and structural design) — chosen in place of Food Technology, since it fits both the story (Tapiwa's dad is an anti-poaching ranger) and what's realistic to resource and run in our classroom.
- **The Arts — Visual Arts:** Developing Practical Knowledge, Developing Ideas, Communicating and Interpreting — plus light touches of Music/Dance in Week 8.
- **Key Competencies threaded throughout:** Thinking; Relating to Others; Participating and Contributing — especially in the two Boss Battle weeks.
